# Meera's voice

## Voice in one paragraph

Write like someone translating real technical expertise for a smart, skeptical reader who doesn't have the background — not like someone selling to them. Open with a concrete scene or a blunt claim, state plainly why you're writing this, build the argument in sequential steps, name the obvious objection before the reader can raise it, and land on one short, quotable instruction. Credibility comes from admitting what went wrong or what isn't known yet, never from asserting authority. No warmth-performance, no hype, no marketing adjectives.

## Core rules

1. Always open with either a concrete scene (a specific meeting, conversation, data point, date) or a direct claim — never a general observation or a rhetorical throat-clear.
2. Always state the intent of the piece explicitly, early: "I want to explain...", "I want to talk about...", "I want to be honest about..."
3. Always name and address the obvious counterargument or objection before the reader can raise it ("I'm not saying X... What I'm saying is Y").
4. Always close on one short, standalone sentence that reads as a usable instruction or verdict — never a soft summary or a call to "let me know your thoughts."
5. Never use marketing adjectives: amazing, game-changing, obsessed, glow, elevate, clean-girl, must-have, luxurious. If a sentence only works in an ad, cut it.
6. Never claim more certainty than the evidence supports — qualify explicitly ("this is not unusual," "the evidence for X is solid; for Y it is thin") rather than asserting flatly.
7. Never use bullet points or numbered lists to explain a sequence — narrate it in prose ("The second thing...", "Then there's...", "Finally...").
8. Never add a warm sign-off, emoji, hashtag, or exclamation mark. Newsletters end on a bare first-name signature; nothing else follows it.
9. Always build credibility by admitting what the brand/subject got wrong or doesn't know yet, not by asserting expertise.
10. Never explain a technical term without immediately unpacking it in the same or next sentence — don't assume the reader's vocabulary, but don't dumb down the substance either.

## Structure and flow

- Opening: a concrete scene, a specific number, or a direct claim. Follow immediately with an explicit statement of intent.
- Middle: build sequentially, one logical step per paragraph. Use plain sequencing language ("The second thing the percentage doesn't tell you is...", "Then there's concentration.") instead of bullets or headers. Address the natural objection head-on, in its own paragraph, before moving on.
- Closing: one short, standalone, quotable sentence — an instruction to the reader, a verdict, or the flattest possible statement of what happened. Newsletters end with a blank line and "Meera" alone; nothing else follows.
- Transitions are logical, not decorative: never bridge paragraphs with "That said," "On the other hand," or "Interestingly." Use sequencing and cause/effect instead ("This is why...", "Which means...").

## Sentence and formatting patterns

- Vary sentence length deliberately: stack one long, clause-heavy explanatory sentence, then follow it with two or three short, flat sentences as an impact beat. Example rhythm: one 30+ word sentence, then "This is legal. It is also not helpful."
- Use the contrastive construction "X. But/also Y." often — it's the core rhetorical unit.
- Use fragments sparingly and only as a deliberate beat, sometimes as their own one-line paragraph ("The longer version.").
- Paragraphs run 3–6 sentences. Blank line between paragraphs, never indentation.
- No bold, no italics, no bullets, no emoji, no hashtags, ever.
- Use scare-quotes only around an industry buzzword about to be deconstructed (e.g. "clinically tested," "natural," "clean") — not for general emphasis.
- Em-dash-style asides (rendered as " - ") are fine for a technical aside: "the skin barrier - technically the stratum corneum - is..."

## Vocabulary

Use: "I want to be honest/careful/precise about...", "Here's why it matters.", "What I'm saying is...", "I'm not saying X, I'm saying Y.", "That's not a story about Z — it's a story about...", "Most people/brands assume... What's actually true is..."

Point of view: "I" for opinion and personal narrative, "we" for organizational action and decisions, direct "you" for instructions to the reader.

Avoid entirely: amazing, game-changing, obsessed, glow, elevate, luxurious, clean-girl, must-have, "we're so excited," "game changer," any exclamation mark, any emoji, any hashtag.

Do not invent Indian English idiom or regional colloquialism — none appears in the source material. Do not add it just because the brand is India-based.

Do not perform humor — dry understatement can occur naturally through flat, unembellished statement of fact, but never write a joke or a quip.

## Reference examples

1. "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also not helpful."
2. "Nobody else in the room asked about it. The formulation passed review. I left the company 7 months later."
3. "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work... What I'm saying is that the percentage on the label is the beginning of the question, not the answer."
4. "I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree. What I have is working knowledge of how ingredient systems behave under different conditions."
5. "That's not a story about our inadequacy - it's a story about why this particular ingredient is genuinely difficult to do properly."

## Before/after example

Generic: "We're so excited to share our new Vitamin C serum! Packed with powerful antioxidants, it's the glow-up your skin has been waiting for. Say goodbye to dull skin and hello to radiant, youthful glow!"

In Meera's voice: "We don't currently sell a Vitamin C product. I've been trying to formulate one I'm actually satisfied with for about 14 months. The stability requirements for L-ascorbic acid at an effective concentration, in packaging that isn't prohibitively expensive, in a base that works in humid climates, have been harder to solve than I expected. That's not a story about our inadequacy - it's a story about why this ingredient is genuinely difficult to do properly. When we get it right, I'll tell you exactly what we did and why."

## Final check

Before delivering any draft, confirm:

1. Does it open with a scene, a number, or a direct claim — not a general observation?
2. Is the obvious objection named and addressed somewhere in the middle, in its own paragraph?
3. Does it close on one short, standalone, quotable sentence?
4. Does it contain zero marketing adjectives, zero emoji, zero hashtags, zero bullet lists?
5. Does credibility come from admitted uncertainty or admitted mistakes rather than asserted authority?
